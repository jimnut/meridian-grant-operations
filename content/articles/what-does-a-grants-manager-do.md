## What is a grant manager?

A grant manager is the person responsible for coordinating a defined part of the grant lifecycle. The title can describe a fundraising role, a financial administration role or a position at a grantmaking organization. This guide focuses on nonprofit grant recipients while identifying where those career paths differ.

Before applying for a job, identify who holds the money and what the role must produce. A nonprofit's manager may prepare proposals and track awarded funds. A foundation's manager may run application processing, due diligence, award agreements and payment administration. A university's manager may support researchers, institutional approvals and sponsored-project accounts.

These are related professions, but their tools, decisions and specialist knowledge are not interchangeable. The [post-award management guide](/resources/post-award-grant-management) explains the recipient-side lifecycle, and the [post-award checklist](/resources/post-award-grant-management-checklist) illustrates work that begins after the award arrives.

## What does a grant manager do?

A grant manager turns funder requirements into work the organization can complete, assigns or coordinates responsibility, and maintains a reliable record of progress. Some positions also lead prospect research and proposal development.

Published employer descriptions, checked September 28, 2026, show the variation. [Columbia University's August 2026 Grants Manager posting](https://opportunities.columbia.edu/en-US/jobs/grants-manager-new-york-united-states) combines proposal preparation, compliance review, budget oversight, reconciliation and reporting. [Shoes That Fit's February 2026 position](https://www.shoesthatfit.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Grants-Manager-STF-job-flyer-2.6.2026.pdf) places more emphasis on foundation fundraising, writing, deadline tracking and coordination with program and finance colleagues. Neither description establishes a universal job specification.

In a recipient organization, the practical responsibilities commonly include:

- Converting an award agreement into a calendar of reports, deliverables and approvals.
- Coordinating proposal or award budgets with finance and program leads.
- Tracking what evidence is needed and who will produce it.
- Preparing narrative reports and coordinating financial schedules.
- Identifying problems early enough to request a decision or funder clarification.
- Preserving submissions, approvals and correspondence through closeout.

The manager does not automatically own every underlying decision. Program staff remain responsible for accurate delivery information. Finance owns accounting records and its assigned financial reviews. Authorized officers sign where the organization or funder requires their authority. The grants manager makes these dependencies visible and follows them through.

## Which skills are most important for a successful grants manager?

The most useful skills are close reading, financial literacy, clear writing, coordination and the judgment to recognize when a matter needs specialist review. Their application changes before and after an award.

| Skill | Pre-award application | Post-award application | Evidence of competence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Requirement analysis | Extract eligibility, scoring criteria and submission instructions | Interpret reporting obligations, restrictions and approval requirements | Requirement register linked to source sections |
| Writing and editing | Build a clear case for the proposed program | Explain results, variances and corrective action accurately | Draft with traceable claims and an appropriate level of detail |
| Budget literacy | Connect proposed activities to justified costs | Reconcile actuals, identify drift and coordinate forecasts | Budget-to-actual schedule with explanations |
| Scheduling | Work backward from an application deadline | Coordinate reporting, renewals and closeout | Calendar showing owners and review dependencies |
| Evidence management | Assemble attachments and source material | Preserve proof of delivery, expenditure and approval | A report figure traceable to its supporting record |
| Relationship management | Clarify funder fit and coordinate contributors | Escalate issues and maintain dependable communication | Concise decision request with options and implications |
| Systems thinking | Reuse approved content without carrying stale facts forward | Maintain consistent identifiers, statuses and version history | A process another colleague can operate |
| Ethical judgment | Avoid exaggerated capacity or invented outcomes | Report unfavorable results honestly and protect sensitive records | Clear separation of facts, forecasts and unresolved questions |

Strong post-award work is not simply proofreading with a calendar. A manager must distinguish the grant period from the organization's fiscal year, a commitment from an expenditure, and an activity count from a verified outcome. The [restricted funds guide](/resources/restricted-funds-management-for-nonprofits) explains why those distinctions matter when interpreting a budget.

For applicable federal awards, [2 CFR 200.329](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.329) places monitoring and reporting responsibilities on recipients and subrecipients. The regulation does not say that giving one employee a grants-manager title transfers all organizational responsibility to that person. A capable manager builds a process around the responsible people. Use the [federal grant compliance checklist](/resources/federal-grant-compliance-checklist-2-cfr-200) to connect that process to the applicable rules and award terms.

## What does a grants manager's working week look like?

A grants manager's week mixes scheduled production with exception handling. The example below allocates a hypothetical 40-hour week; it is an editorial planning example, not a survey, staffing standard or account of a real employee.

| Work block | Illustrative hours | Useful output |
|---|---|---|
| Award and deadline review | 4 | Prioritized obligations, owners and issues needing decisions |
| Program coordination and evidence review | 8 | Verified measures and missing-record follow-up |
| Finance coordination and budget review | 7 | Reconciled figures, variance explanations and open questions |
| Report or proposal preparation | 10 | Reviewed draft ready for the next approval |
| Funder communication and approvals | 4 | Documented decisions, clarifications and correspondence |
| Records, workflow maintenance and learning | 3 | Updated files, procedures and source references |
| Unscheduled issues and contingency | 4 | Capacity for corrections, urgent requests or changed deadlines |
| Total | 40 | A planning allocation, not an expected productivity benchmark |

Monday might begin with the next month's obligations and a short finance check. Tuesday and Wednesday may focus on evidence and report drafting. Thursday can be reserved for review and questions that require funder clarification. Friday provides space to confirm submissions, update records and prepare the next handoff.

Actual timing will vary with reporting cycles and application deadlines. A week with several final reports may need a different allocation or temporary support. The point of a weekly plan is to expose that conflict early, not to force every job into the same calendar.

The [grant reporting requirements guide](/resources/grant-reporting-requirements) explains the report package; the [grant reporting calendar template](/resources/grant-reporting-calendar-template) helps separate the funder's deadline from the earlier dates for evidence, finance review and approval. A calendar with only the final due date conceals most of the work.

## What is the salary range for a grants manager?

Pay varies by employer, location, sector and scope. Compare dated postings for similar jobs with a clearly labeled occupational benchmark. The figures below are published source data in US dollars, checked September 28, 2026, not estimates.

| Source and date | Published pay | What the number represents |
|---|---|---|
| Shoes That Fit, February 6, 2026 | $65,000–$72,000 annual salary | Advertised Grants Manager range in Claremont, California; foundation fundraising emphasis |
| Columbia University, opening August 17, 2026 | $75,000–$85,000 annual salary | Advertised Grants Manager range in New York; pre- and post-award research administration; 35-hour workweek listed |
| BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, May 2025 wage data | $72,550 median annual wage | National occupation of fundraisers, broader than grants managers |

The employer ranges come from the [Shoes That Fit position notice](https://www.shoesthatfit.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/02/Grants-Manager-STF-job-flyer-2.6.2026.pdf) and [Columbia's posting](https://opportunities.columbia.edu/en-US/jobs/grants-manager-new-york-united-states). They describe advertised ranges, not accepted offers or a representative sample of the profession. These notices are pay examples, not confirmation that either vacancy remains open.

The [BLS fundraiser profile](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/business-and-financial/fundraisers.htm) reports that the lowest-paid 10 percent earned below $46,630 and the highest-paid 10 percent earned above $115,820 in May 2025. These are percentile boundaries for fundraisers, not a recommended grants-manager salary band. A post-award financial administration role may fit that benchmark poorly.

When evaluating an offer, compare the actual responsibilities, expected hours, benefits, location expectations and decision authority. A position covering proposals, accounting coordination, federal subrecipient monitoring and board reporting should not be benchmarked solely against a writing-focused role with the same title.

## Which associations and credentials fit grant professionals?

Choose an association by the work you do and the education or peer support it provides. GPA, NGMA and PEAK serve overlapping but distinct parts of the grants profession; a credential issuer is a separate type of organization.

| Organization or credential | What its official materials describe | Career fit to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| Grant Professionals Association, GPA | Professional association dedicated to grant professionals | Grant research, proposal development, post-award learning and peer community |
| Grant Professionals Certification Institute, GPCI | Administrator of the Grant Professional Certified credential | Experienced grant professionals considering a competency-based credential |
| National Grants Management Association, NGMA | Education and the Certified Grants Management Specialist credential | Grants administration across government, nonprofits, education and other sectors |
| PEAK Grantmaking | Education on grantmaking operations | Grants managers working for funder organizations |

The [GPA's official description](https://grantprofessionals.org/) identifies its professional-association focus. Review current training and chapter offerings against the work you need to learn. Membership is not itself evidence that a person holds a certification.

[GPCI's GPC eligibility guidance](https://grantcredential.org/the-exam/eligibility/) describes an experienced-professional credential, not an entry-level qualification. If you are early in your career, first establish which experiences count and what preparation is needed. GPCI also explains why a [course completion certificate is distinct from professional certification](https://grantcredential.org/why-certify/certification-vs-certificate-of-completion/).

[NGMA describes CGMS](https://www.ngma.org/certification/) as recognition of competency across the grants-management lifecycle. Its scope includes public, nonprofit and educational settings. That makes its body of knowledge worth examining for roles involving award administration; the current eligibility and exam requirements should drive a certification plan.

[PEAK's introductory materials](https://learning.peakgrantmaking.org/products/gm101-introduction-to-grants-management) focus on the grantmaking process within funder organizations. A nonprofit recipient can learn from funder practice, but that perspective differs from managing restricted funds inside a grantee organization.

No membership or credential guarantees a job, a salary increase or compliance with an award. Compare course content and professional community first, then decide whether the cost and time match your career path.

## How does the role change from three grants to thirty?

As a portfolio grows, the manager spends more effort designing consistent handoffs, supervising exceptions and keeping records understandable across teams. Grant count alone does not establish a safe workload: reporting frequency, subawards and financial complexity can matter more.

With three straightforward awards, one person may maintain the register and directly coordinate each report. They still need a backup and clear finance review. With thirty varied awards, relying on one person's memory becomes harder to sustain. Program owners need their own responsibilities, finance needs repeatable reconciliation and leadership needs a short decision list.

| Small, relatively simple portfolio | Larger or more complex portfolio |
|---|---|
| Manager can review each award individually | Review needs consistent categories and an exception view |
| Informal handoffs may be easy to follow | Handoffs need named owners, review dates and records |
| One shared file may be practical | Access, version history and workload visibility become more important |
| Backup can learn a limited set of agreements | Written procedures and cross-training become central |

The [multiple-grant tracking guide](/resources/track-multiple-grants-without-missing-deadlines) describes a repeatable routine for a growing portfolio. For documentation, the [audit-ready grant file guide](/resources/audit-ready-grant-file) shows what a colleague should be able to reconstruct without interviewing the original manager.

## How can a new grants manager demonstrate readiness?

Demonstrate readiness with a small, accurate work sample and a clear explanation of how you would coordinate decisions. An anonymized or synthetic award exercise is more useful than a list of software names.

Prepare an obligation register, a short reporting calendar and a budget-to-actual explanation using a public sample or invented organization. Label invented amounts and dates. Show how a report claim connects to evidence and where a finance or funder decision is needed.

For an interview, ask what proportion of the role is pre-award, who signs reports, how financial reconciliation works and what support exists during reporting peaks. Ask which decisions the manager can make independently. Those answers reveal whether the role is primarily writing, coordination, financial administration or a combination.

A strong handoff is a useful test of professional maturity: another authorized person should be able to identify the next obligation, locate the governing requirement and see what remains unresolved. That ability serves the organization throughout applications, [report preparation](/grant-reporting-software-for-nonprofits) and final closeout.
