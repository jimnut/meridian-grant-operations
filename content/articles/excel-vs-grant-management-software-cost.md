## Is grant management software worth it for a small nonprofit?

It can be worth it when the team spends enough time maintaining trackers, following up on evidence and rebuilding reports to justify the full cost of a system. The useful comparison is your current process against a working replacement with real staff participation.

This model is for nonprofit grant recipients choosing how to manage active awards. It does not estimate fundraising revenue, a probability of winning grants or a monetary value for every operational risk. Those questions require different evidence.

Start by separating three things: cash spending, staff capacity and control quality. A subscription changes cash spending. Removing repetitive work releases capacity. Making approvals and evidence easier to inspect can improve control quality. They can support the same decision without being interchangeable measures.

For example, a salaried grants manager who spends fewer hours chasing files still receives the same salary. The organization has more time available for program coordination or reporting. Call that capacity value until it reduces an actual expense or avoids a planned cost.

## What assumptions should a grant management cost model use?

A useful model needs the number of active grants, hours spent maintaining each grant, the employer cost of that time, software prices and the work that remains after implementation. Every input below is either an explicitly labeled assumption or a named public price or labor benchmark. The worked model uses US dollars.

| Input | Base model | Status and reason |
|---|---|---|
| Active grants | 5, 10 or 25 throughout the year | Hypothetical portfolio sizes, held constant for comparability |
| Spreadsheet upkeep | 2 hours per grant each month | Assumption; test a range of 1–3 hours |
| Work retained with software | 0.75 hours per grant each month | Assumption; source review, reconciliation and follow-up remain |
| Organization-wide software administration | 1 hour each month | Assumption for access, configuration and exception review |
| Initial setup and training | 12 staff hours once | Assumption for this example; not a vendor implementation promise |
| Employer compensation cost | $46.89 for each hour worked | BLS June 2026 private-industry average, including full- and part-time workers; a proxy |
| Incremental spreadsheet license | $0 | Assumes the organization already pays for suitable spreadsheet access |
| Software subscription | Appropriate published annual plan | Prices checked September 26, 2026; taxes and special discounts excluded |

The labor proxy comes from the [Bureau of Labor Statistics' June 2026 employer compensation release](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/ecec_09092026.htm). Released September 9, 2026, it reports $32.82 in wages and salaries plus $14.07 in benefits per hour worked for private-industry workers, totaling $46.89. This is a broad employer-compensation measure, not a nonprofit grants-manager salary survey. It includes benefits but is not a complete overhead allocation rate.

Replace the proxy with your finance team's estimate when possible. Use compensation and employer-paid benefits divided by the hours relevant to the analysis, consistently for both options. If several people do the work, calculate a weighted rate or separate their hours. Do not add benefits a second time to a rate that already includes them.

This compares an existing tracker with newly adopted software. Add workbook setup if neither option is established. Existing spreadsheet access is assumed to continue under both options; its common license cost cancels rather than becoming an invented saving.

The assumed two hours covers tracking obligations, updating budget summaries, checking file links and preparing recurring status views. It excludes delivering the funded program, substantive accounting judgments and writing the report narrative. Those tasks do not vanish when a spreadsheet is replaced.

## What does spreadsheet upkeep cost for 5, 10 and 25 grants?

In the illustrative base model, monthly upkeep costs $93.78 per active grant: 2 hours × $46.89. Multiply that by 12 months and the number of grants to estimate annual staff capacity committed to the tracker.

For five grants, the working is 5 × 2 × $46.89 × 12 = $5,626.80. For ten grants it is 10 × 2 × $46.89 × 12 = $11,253.60. For twenty-five grants it is 25 × 2 × $46.89 × 12 = $28,134.00.

| Active grants | Upkeep hours each month | Annual staff capacity value | Incremental spreadsheet license assumed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 10 | $5,626.80 | $0 |
| 10 | 20 | $11,253.60 | $0 |
| 25 | 50 | $28,134.00 | $0 |

These totals are modeled, not observed results. A disciplined five-grant workbook may need much less time. A portfolio with multiple budgets, subrecipients and funder portals may need more. Count actual work for a full reporting cycle and record exceptional months separately.

## What changes when you include software setup and retained work?

Software has both a subscription cost and staff work. In this example, use Starter for 5 or 10 grants and Growth for 25 grants, assuming each plan's other limits fit the team.

The annual software-process total is:

`subscription + (grants × 0.75 × $46.89 × 12) + (1 × $46.89 × 12)`

For five grants, that is $780 + $2,110.05 + $562.68 = $3,452.73 in recurring annual cost and capacity value. Initial setup adds 12 × $46.89 = $562.68, making the first-year total $4,015.41.

| Active grants | Annual spreadsheet model | Annual software process, after setup | First-year software process | First-year difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | $5,626.80 | $3,452.73 | $4,015.41 | $1,611.39 |
| 10 | $11,253.60 | $5,562.78 | $6,125.46 | $5,128.14 |
| 25 | $28,134.00 | $12,900.93 | $13,463.61 | $14,670.39 |

For ten grants, recurring work is 10 × 0.75 × $46.89 × 12 = $4,220.10; add $780 and $562.68. For twenty-five, recurring work is 25 × 0.75 × $46.89 × 12 = $10,550.25; add $1,788 and $562.68. Add the same $562.68 initial setup assumption to each first-year figure.

The positive difference is potential net capacity value under these assumptions. It is not a claim that GrantConsole users achieve these results. If data entry must be duplicated or the team maintains its old tracker indefinitely, retained work could exceed the model.

## At what grant count does software break even?

Under the base assumptions, GrantConsole Starter breaks even at two active grants after setup and three in the first year. Those results describe this model only; they are not a recommendation to migrate every three-grant organization.

Each grant releases an assumed 2 − 0.75 = 1.25 hours each month. The annual value is 1.25 × $46.89 × 12 = $703.35 per grant. Recurring fixed costs are $780 subscription + $562.68 administration = $1,342.68. Divide $1,342.68 by $703.35 to obtain 1.91 grants, rounded upward to two.

In year one, include setup: ($780 + $562.68 + $562.68) ÷ $703.35 = 2.71, rounded upward to three. This calculation is valid only while Starter's grant and editor limits fit.

Change the time assumption and the decision changes sharply. If the spreadsheet takes one hour and software still needs 0.75, the difference is only 0.25 hours. First-year break-even becomes $1,905.36 ÷ (0.25 × $46.89 × 12) = 13.54 grants. That exceeds Starter's ten-grant limit, so its result cannot be used. Recalculate with Growth: ($1,788 + $562.68 + $562.68) ÷ $140.67 = 20.71, or 21 grants.

If measured time removed is zero or negative, this time-value model has no positive break-even point. Compare other control benefits separately.

The most important input is measured work removed. A lower price cannot rescue a system that adds more administration than it removes.

## What are the non-monetary costs of a spreadsheet?

Spreadsheet risks include unclear ownership, broken evidence links, inconsistent versions and fragile formulas. They matter when they affect the organization's ability to meet an obligation or explain a reported result, not simply because a file is called Excel.

For applicable federal awards, [2 CFR 200.339](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.339) permits remedies including withheld payments, disallowed costs and suspension or termination when the agency or pass-through entity determines that specific conditions cannot remedy noncompliance. A missed deadline does not automatically trigger every remedy. Confirm your award's applicable terms; these sections were checked September 26 against the official eCFR snapshot current through September 24.

Likewise, [section 200.403](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.403) requires adequate cost documentation. Installing software does not make an unsupported expense allowable. A well-controlled spreadsheet can preserve an excellent record; an unmaintained database can preserve a poor one.

Use a simple control comparison: can another staff member locate the approved budget, identify the next report owner and reproduce the submitted total? The [audit-ready grant file guide](/resources/audit-ready-grant-file) sets out the evidence trail. Keep financial exposure out of the savings model unless you have a defensible basis for estimating it.

## When is a spreadsheet the right answer?

A spreadsheet can be sensible for a small portfolio with one responsible maintainer, limited reporting variation and a reliable backup—for example, two straightforward awards. A portfolio without federal funding may have fewer regulatory demands, but private funder agreements can still be complex.

Grant count is a screening signal. One complicated federal award with subrecipients can require more coordination than ten small foundation grants. Conversely, a standardized portfolio may work well with a controlled workbook and established finance processes.

Keep the spreadsheet if it answers the important operational questions accurately, costs little to maintain and survives staff absence. Protect formulas, define status values, use a shared location and review the tracker alongside accounting records. See [deadline tracking alternatives](/resources/track-grant-deadlines-without-spreadsheets) before deciding that buying a dedicated product is necessary.

## What are the best Excel templates for grant tracking?

The best template for your team separates award records, obligations, budgets and evidence links instead of putting everything in one long notes cell. It should expose the next action without requiring its creator to explain the workbook.

Use a stable grant identifier across tabs. Store reporting-period dates separately from deadlines. Give each obligation a named owner and link to the source requirement. For financial controls, use the approach in [restricted funds management](/resources/restricted-funds-management-for-nonprofits), with accounting records remaining the basis for reported expenditure.

The [free grant management spreadsheet template](/resources/grant-management-spreadsheet-template) provides a five-tab starting point with fictional examples and documented row limits. A template is useful when its instructions let the next maintainer follow the structure. Avoid a complicated dashboard whose formulas nobody can inspect. Test it using one real award before loading the full portfolio.

## How do you test the business case before committing?

Test one complete workflow: enter an award, assign a report, attach supporting evidence, reconcile its budget and prepare the reporting packet. Measure staff time for the same tasks in both systems. Include correcting mistakes and helping colleagues use the tool.
