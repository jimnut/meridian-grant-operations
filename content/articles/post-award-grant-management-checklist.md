## Who this checklist is for

For the definition, recipient responsibilities and software categories behind these steps, start with the [post-award grant management guide](/resources/post-award-grant-management).

This checklist is for the people who inherit a grant after the celebration: grants and development managers who own the calendar, finance leads who own the restricted budget, program staff who produce the deliverables, and executive directors who have to answer "are we on top of our grants?" It assumes you are a nonprofit **recipient**, not a grantmaker, whether you manage one complex award or several simpler ones. Use it with a maintained spreadsheet or software; the important test is whether responsibilities and records remain reliable.

For federal awards, read the applicable version of [2 CFR part 200](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/part-200), agency implementation and award terms together. Reporting, closeout and audit guidance was reviewed on October 18, 2026; individual source-check dates are listed below. This is an operational checklist, not a complete assessment of every program. The [federal grant compliance checklist](/resources/federal-grant-compliance-checklist-2-cfr-200) connects specific requirements to records and review work. For private awards, check the agreement and applicable law rather than importing federal rules automatically.

## The checklist

Work through the eight stages in order for each new award, then use the monthly rhythm at the end to keep the whole portfolio current.

### 1. Accept and set up the award

- [ ] File the fully executed award document, the approved budget and the approved proposal or scope in one place per grant.
- [ ] Record the period of performance start and end dates, the total award, cost-share commitments and any pre-award cost approvals.
- [ ] List every restriction: purpose, budget categories, indirect cost rate, unallowable items, publicity or acknowledgment requirements, and any special conditions.
- [ ] Identify the funder contacts — for federal awards, the grants management officer and program officer named in the award; for foundations, the program officer and grants administrator.
- [ ] Set up the award in your accounting system so expenditures can be identified by grant. 2 CFR 200.302 requires that federal award records "sufficiently identify the amount, source, and expenditure of Federal funds" and support "comparison of expenditures with budget amounts for each Federal award."

*Why:* everything later depends on knowing exactly what was promised and what is restricted. Grants.gov's own advice is to clarify terms with your grants and program officers early rather than "submit a report and wait for problems to be identified."

### 2. Build the obligation calendar and assign owners

- [ ] Enter every reporting deadline (financial and performance), deliverable date, site visit, renewal or reapplication window and the period-of-performance end date.
- [ ] Give each obligation one accountable internal owner, with reviewers and backup coverage recorded separately.
- [ ] Choose internal lead times, such as a review two weeks before a report and a pre-closeout review 30 days before the period ends. These are planning examples; adapt them to the work.
- [ ] Enter the award-specific reporting dates. [2 CFR 200.328](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.328) and [200.329](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.329) generally set annual and quarterly/semiannual outside limits of 90 and 30 calendar days. Check specific conditions, performance-report exceptions, any earlier award date and approved extensions instead of treating those limits as your schedule.

*Why:* a date is more useful when another team member can find its source, owner and review plan. The [reporting calendar CSV](/resources/grant-reporting-calendar-template) provides a simple starting structure.

### 3. Set up the restricted budget

- [ ] Enter the approved budget by category as the plan for the grant.
- [ ] Compare spending with the approved budget and expected delivery schedule over the *grant period*. In a hypothetical award, 65% spent at 40% of time elapsed is a 25-percentage-point timing difference. An approved early equipment purchase could explain it; the difference alone does not establish overspending or noncompliance.
- [ ] Set review triggers appropriate to the spending plan and decide who follows up. A ±15-percentage-point difference is an illustrative screening threshold, not a federal standard or an allowability test.
- [ ] Confirm which budget changes need funder approval before you make them (see stage 6).

*Why:* spending, commitments and the forecast for remaining work can reveal a funding gap or an unexplained balance. Comparing spend with elapsed time prompts a review; the approved spending profile determines how to interpret it. The [restricted funds guide](/resources/restricted-funds-management-for-nonprofits) explains the calculation.

### 4. Design the evidence system

- [ ] For each deliverable and each report, list the evidence that will prove it: attendance records, invoices, timesheets, photos, survey results, deliverable files, sign-offs.
- [ ] Decide where evidence is stored and how it is named so that anyone on the team can find it without the person who created it.
- [ ] Attach evidence to the deliverable it supports as it is produced, not the week the report is due.
- [ ] Two weeks before any report, check the required evidence is present and readable.

*Why:* 2 CFR 200.329 asks performance reports for "a comparison of accomplishments to the objectives of the Federal award" and explanations where goals were not met. That comparison is only credible when the evidence already exists.

### 5. Run the reporting cadence

- [ ] Draft financial reports from the accounting system, reconciled to the approved budget, with cost share and program income shown where required.
- [ ] Draft performance reports that compare accomplishments to objectives, explain any shortfall, and address cost overruns or higher-than-expected unit costs (2 CFR 200.329).
- [ ] Route each report through an internal reviewer before submission — typically finance reviews program numbers and program reviews financial narrative.
- [ ] Submit through the funder's required channel and file proof of submission (confirmation email, portal receipt) with the report.
- [ ] Notify the funder between reports if a significant development threatens the objectives; 2 CFR 200.329 requires recipients to report problems, delays or adverse conditions along with a corrective plan.

*Why:* the report should let a reviewer trace reported results to financial and program support. File its approval and submission evidence alongside the underlying records.

### 6. Watch for changes that need prior approval

For federal awards, [2 CFR 200.308](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.308) lists prior-approval requirements and specific waiver provisions. Check the applicable rule and award terms before implementing a change. Common triggers include:

- [ ] A change in the scope or objective of the project, even without a budget change.
- [ ] A change in key personnel named in the award, or the project director disengaging for more than three months or reducing effort by 25% or more.
- [ ] Transfers of funds budgeted for participant support costs to other categories.
- [ ] Subaward activities not proposed and approved in the award.
- [ ] Changes to the approved cost-sharing amount, or a need for additional federal funds.
- [ ] A no-cost extension unless the agency has authorized the one-time extension described in 200.308(g)(2). That authority is conditional: notify the agency with justification and the revised period at least 10 calendar days before the end; do not use it solely to spend an unobligated balance. A prohibited extension, additional funding or a scope change requires prior approval.

For foundation grants, look for clauses on budget reallocation thresholds, use of unspent funds, changes in staffing and extension requests. When in doubt, ask in writing and file the answer.

### 7. Close out on time

- [ ] Schedule a pre-closeout review before the end date, using 30 days as a planning example and starting earlier for complex awards. List open deliverables, unspent balances, outstanding invoices and any evidence still missing.
- [ ] Submit final reports and liquidate financial obligations within the applicable deadline. [2 CFR 200.344](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.344) gives recipients 120 calendar days after the award period ends and subrecipients 90 days after the subaward period ends, or an earlier agreed date. Record any justified extension only when authorized.
- [ ] Return or account for unobligated balances as the award requires, and reconcile cost share and program income.
- [ ] Complete equipment and property dispositions if the award funded them.
- [ ] Record the closeout date and confirmation from the funder.

The [grant closeout checklist](/resources/grant-closeout-checklist) covers the federal and private branches in detail.

### 8. Retain records and prepare for audit

- [ ] Assign retention triggers to the grant file. [2 CFR 200.334](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.334) generally uses three years from submission of the final financial report, but quarterly or annually renewed awards use their respective reports. Property, program income and indirect-cost records have separate rules; check written extensions and unresolved proceedings before disposal.
- [ ] Track entity-wide federal expenditures for the fiscal year and assess the applicable audit requirement under [2 CFR 200.501](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.501). The [Federal Audit Clearinghouse](https://www.fac.gov/audit-resources/submission-guide/starting/) identifies the $1,000,000 threshold for fiscal years beginning on or after October 1, 2024, and $750,000 for earlier fiscal years. Confirm Subpart F applicability and eligibility for a program-specific audit.
- [ ] Plan audit submission under [2 CFR 200.512](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.512): generally the **earlier of 30 calendar days after receipt of the auditor's reports or nine months after the audit period ends**. Check the section's authorized-extension and nonbusiness-day rules.
- [ ] Set private-grant retention from the agreement, applicable law and the organization's retention policy. If the agreement is silent, obtain qualified advice on the relevant records and holds; do not assume the federal period resolves every obligation.

## Federal vs. foundation grants: what changes

| Item | Federal award (2 CFR 200) | Foundation or corporate grant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reporting cadence | Generally annual to quarterly; check specific conditions and performance-report exceptions | Set by the agreement |
| Interim report deadline | Generally 90/30-day outside limits; check actual award dates and authorized exceptions | Set by the agreement |
| Final reports and liquidation | Recipient: 120 days; subrecipient: 90 or earlier agreed, with authorized extensions | Agreement and authorized changes |
| Budget changes | Prior approval triggers listed in 200.308 | Reallocation thresholds vary; ask if unstated |
| Record retention | Generally three years; category-specific triggers, extensions and exceptions | Agreement, applicable law and retention policy |
| Audit exposure | Assess Subpart F applicability, expenditure threshold and audit-period rules | Agreement and other applicable audit requirements |

## How to run the checklist month to month

Stages 1 through 4 happen once per award. Stages 5 through 8 repeat, and they are where portfolios drift. A workable rhythm for a team managing 5–25 grants:

1. **Weekly, 15 minutes:** everything due in the next 30 days, its owner, and whether the evidence is ready.
2. **Monthly, one hour with finance:** percent spent versus percent of period elapsed for every active grant, any grant outside tolerance, prior-approval questions, and grants entering their last 90 days.
3. **Quarterly, with leadership:** reports submitted on time, closeouts completed, renewals decided, and anything an auditor would flag.

Write the outcome of each review down. The habit of recording "who decided what, when" is most of what an audit trail is.

## Common mistakes this checklist prevents

- Treating the calendar as the system. Dates without owners, lead time and evidence checks still get missed.
- Measuring spending against the fiscal year instead of the grant period, so overspending is discovered at closeout.
- Collecting evidence at report time, when the people who have it have moved on.
- Making budget or personnel changes first and asking the funder afterward.
- Letting a grant with no owner sit in "active" status for months.
- Discarding files three years after the grant *ended* rather than three years after the *final financial report* was submitted.

Print the checklist, adapt the foundation column to your funders' actual agreements, and review it on the rhythm above.
