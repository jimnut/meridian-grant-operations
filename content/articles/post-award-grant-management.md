## What is post-award grant management?

Post-award grant management turns an accepted award into a controlled delivery process. For a nonprofit recipient, the central question is whether the organization is doing what it promised, spending within the applicable rules and keeping enough evidence to demonstrate both.

This guide explains the category, its responsibilities and its software requirements for US nonprofit recipients. For a task-by-task implementation list, use the separate [post-award grant management checklist](/resources/post-award-grant-management-checklist).

Three roles are easy to confuse. A **grantseeker** identifies opportunities and prepares applications. A **grantmaker** evaluates applications, makes awards and oversees the organizations it funds. A **grantee**, or recipient, runs the funded work and accounts for the award. The same nonprofit can occupy all three roles, but receiving money and distributing money create different operational needs.

Post-award work also extends beyond the day the program finishes. Reports, outstanding payments, property questions, funder follow-up and records can remain open after service delivery ends. Marking a project complete does not automatically close its award.

## What is grant lifecycle management?

Grant lifecycle management coordinates the work from opportunity assessment through award delivery and closeout. [Grants.gov describes three main phases](https://www.grants.gov/learn-grants/grants-101/the-grant-lifecycle): pre-award, award and post-award, with closeout included in post-award. The four-part operating view below separates closeout to make its handoff easier to manage; it is not a different federal definition.

| Operating phase | What happens | What the applicant or grantee owes | Who usually coordinates it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-award | Opportunity assessment, application and funder review | Accurate eligibility information, a feasible scope and a supportable proposed budget | Development or grants lead, with program and finance input |
| Award | Terms are clarified, the award is accepted and records are set up | Authorized acceptance, understanding of conditions and a workable delivery plan | Authorized signatory and grants lead |
| Post-award | Implementation, spending, monitoring, reporting and approved changes | Delivery, financial accountability, evidence, timely submissions and required notifications | Program lead, finance lead and grants lead together |
| Closeout | Final reporting, settlement, funder review and retention setup | Required final documents, resolution of balances and continuing records responsibilities | Grants lead coordinating finance and program owners |

The crucial transition is from a proposed project to an approved one. Do not populate the operating plan from an early application if the funder approved a smaller amount, a different timetable or additional conditions. The accepted award and subsequent amendments must govern the working record.

Grant periods can differ from an organization's fiscal year. An award running March through February needs a view across those dates, even if the organization closes its books in June. Keep both views: one for the award's performance and reporting, another for organizational accounts and audit planning.

## What happens after you receive a grant?

After receiving an award, confirm what has actually been authorized and translate the terms into records your team can operate. Receipt of an award letter, acceptance, permission to incur costs and receipt of cash are distinct events; check the agreement for their relationship.

The initial handoff should produce five things:

1. **An authoritative award file.** Keep the executed agreement, approved scope, budget, conditions and amendments together. Record who can approve changes and where formal notices must go.
2. **An obligation register.** Give each report, deliverable and approval request its own record, owner and date. Keep the reporting period separate from the submission deadline.
3. **A financial mapping.** Connect the award to accounting codes and approved budget categories. Document how expenses, cost share and program income will be reconciled where applicable.
4. **An evidence plan.** Define the records needed to support financial entries and program results, who produces them and when they become available.
5. **A review and escalation routine.** Decide who resolves missing information, who reviews submissions and when a problem needs funder contact.

For federal awards, these records support explicit requirements. [2 CFR 200.302](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.302) requires identifiable award records, source documentation and financial reporting capability. Its budget requirement is concise: **“Comparison of expenditures with budget amounts for each Federal award.”** That is a comparison for each award, not merely the organization's total budget.

The same system needs documented controls. [2 CFR 200.303](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.303) requires recipients and subrecipients to establish, document and maintain effective internal control over federal awards. A calendar is one useful control; it does not supply the entire system.

Consider a fictional youth-services grant with a September 30 reporting period end and an October 30 submission deadline. The program lead must deliver reviewed service counts; finance must complete the relevant reconciliation; an authorized person must approve the submission. One calendar entry saying “report due” hides all three dependencies. Separate records make the handoff visible before October 30 arrives.

## What is grant administration?

Grant administration is the coordination of the award's formal records, requirements, communications and decisions. It often spans the lifecycle, while post-award administration focuses on awards already accepted.

A grants administrator may maintain the agreement, prepare reporting calendars, coordinate budget revisions, assemble submissions and preserve approvals. That role should not become the default owner of every missing receipt, service count or spending decision. Assign the grants lead responsibility for coordination, finance responsibility for financial accuracy and the program lead responsibility for delivery and performance evidence.

Use a handoff record that answers four questions: what is required, who prepares it, who approves it and what proves completion. Record submission and retain its receipt. Track funder acceptance and requests for revision as separate states.

Small nonprofits may combine roles in one person. Even then, distinguish preparation, review and authorization in the process. If staffing makes independent review difficult, leadership and a qualified adviser can help design proportionate controls. Buying software does not settle that organizational decision.

## What reports and evidence does post-award management require?

Post-award management requires the reports and supporting records specified by the award and applicable rules. Financial, performance and closeout reporting answer different questions; an expense summary cannot substitute for evidence that the project achieved its objectives.

| Record or report | Question it answers | Practical preparation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Financial report | What happened to the award's funds? | Reconcile the report to financial records, the approved budget and applicable reporting instructions |
| Performance progress report | What work and results were achieved? | Compare results with objectives, explain shortfalls and retain the underlying measurement records |
| Approval or amendment file | What changes were authorized? | Retain the request, written decision, effective date and revised baseline |
| Final closeout package | What remains to settle or document? | Assemble final reports, balances, property information when applicable, and submission receipts |

Federal financial reporting uses OMB-approved government-wide data elements under [2 CFR 200.328](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.328), which identifies the Federal Financial Report, or [SF-425](https://www.grants.gov/forms/form-items-description/fid/751). Follow the agency's instructions and reporting system rather than assuming a downloaded blank form is the required submission channel.

Performance reports follow the award's format. [2 CFR 200.329](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.329) calls for comparison with objectives and applicable explanations of missed goals or cost overruns. The [Research Performance Progress Report](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/research-performance-progress-reports), or RPPR, is a standardized format for research and related awards; it is not the universal performance form for every nonprofit program.

Under sections 200.328 and 200.329, regular financial and performance reporting is generally at least annual and no more frequent than quarterly unless specific conditions apply. Annual reports must be due no later than 90 calendar days after the reporting period; quarterly and semiannual reports no later than 30 days. Enter the deadline actually assigned to your award, including any justified extension, and leave internal preparation time before it.

Final financial and performance reports are separate obligations. [2 CFR 200.344](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.344) sets the general recipient deadline at 120 calendar days after the period of performance and the subrecipient deadline at 90 days, or an earlier date agreed with the pass-through entity. There is no single universal “closeout form” that replaces all required final reports.

Evidence should be relevant and retrievable. A financial entry needs its supporting source record; a claimed participant count needs a reproducible counting method and the appropriate underlying record. Limit access to sensitive information. The [audit-ready grant file guide](/resources/audit-ready-grant-file) explains how to organize the document trail without treating every shared folder as an unrestricted evidence store.

## Which federal rules matter after the award?

For awards subject to the Uniform Guidance, start with 2 CFR part 200 and the award's agency-specific terms. Three parts of the rulebook are particularly useful for orienting a nonprofit team:

- **Subpart D, post-federal award requirements:** financial management, internal controls, reporting, changes, records and closeout.
- **Subpart E, cost principles:** the framework for evaluating costs. [Section 200.403](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.403) includes necessity, reasonableness, allocability, consistent treatment and adequate documentation. An available budget balance alone does not establish allowability.
- **Subpart F, audit requirements:** whether and how federal audit requirements apply. Under [section 200.501](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.501), the current expenditure threshold is $1,000,000 in federal awards during the entity's fiscal year, subject to the section's conditions and applicable implementation dates. It is not a threshold based on the face value of one award.

Changes need their own decision path. For example, a scope or objective change requires prior written approval under [2 CFR 200.308](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.308), even when it does not require a budget revision. A dashboard warning should trigger review; it should not automatically rewrite the approved budget.

Closeout also starts a records question. [2 CFR 200.334](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-2/section-200.334) generally measures three-year retention from submission of the final financial report, with separate rules and exceptions for renewed awards, property, unresolved proceedings and other records. Record the applicable trigger and any hold, not simply “delete three years after the project ends.”

Foundation and corporate awards do not automatically inherit those federal deadlines. Read their agreements and applicable law. Ask a qualified grants adviser, accountant or attorney to resolve uncertain interpretations, particularly for cost allocation, prior approval or audit applicability.

## Which software category supports post-award grant management?

Choose software by the work you need to perform and whose awards it organizes. Product categories overlap, so a vendor's name alone does not tell you which features are available in the plan you are considering.

| Category | Primary job and verified examples | What a recipient should check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Funding discovery | Locate and assess funding opportunities; [GrantForward](https://www.grantforward.com/subscription_plans) describes funding discovery and dissemination for research institutions | Whether accepted-award budgets, evidence and closeout are covered, rather than assuming saved opportunities are an operating portfolio |
| Grantmaker platform | Run applications, reviews, awards and grantee reporting; [Submittable](https://www.submittable.com/solutions/grants) describes this grantmaker workflow | Whether access is controlled by one funder and whether the recipient can manage unrelated awards together |
| Recipient post-award tools | Coordinate accepted awards, spending, obligations and evidence; dedicated tools and broader lifecycle suites can serve this job | Whether the actual plan handles your accounting handoffs, evidence relationships, permissions and export needs |
